2016 · Science

Can apes anticipate another’s mistaken belief?

Great apes anticipate that other individuals will act according to false beliefs ↗

While watching a video, great apes looked ahead to where an actor mistakenly believed an object was hidden. Their anticipatory gaze provides evidence relevant to the evolutionary origins of false-belief understanding.

Authors and citation

Christopher Krupenye, Fumihiro Kano, Satoshi Hirata, Josep Call, Michael Tomasello

2019 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences

Using personal experience to infer what another can see

Great apes use self-experience to anticipate an agent’s action in a false-belief test ↗

Apes first experienced whether a screen was see-through or opaque, then watched identical videos. Their different gaze predictions suggest they used that experience to infer the actor’s visual access, testing explanations beyond simple responses to observed actions.

Authors and citation

Fumihiro Kano, Christopher Krupenye, Satoshi Hirata, Masaki Tomonaga, Josep Call

Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 116(42), 20904–20909. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1910095116 ↗

2015 · Current Biology

Remembering an event to anticipate what comes next

Great Apes Make Anticipatory Looks Based on Long-Term Memory of Single Events ↗

After seeing a film once, chimpanzees and bonobos watched it again 24 hours later and looked ahead to the location or object involved in an upcoming event. Their gaze showed that they retained information from a single viewing and used it to anticipate what would happen next.

Authors and citation

Fumihiro Kano, Satoshi Hirata

Current Biology 25(19), 2513–2517. DOI: 10.1016/j.cub.2015.08.004 ↗

2014 · Psychological Science

Anticipating the goal of another’s action

Great Apes Generate Goal-Based Action Predictions: An Eye-Tracking Study ↗

After watching a human hand grasp an object, great apes looked ahead to that same object even when its position was swapped. The same prediction was absent for a mechanical claw, supporting goal-based anticipation of human actions in this task.

Authors and citation

Fumihiro Kano, Josep Call

Psychological Science 25(9), 1691–1698. DOI: 10.1177/0956797614536402 ↗

2023 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences

Remembering social partners after years apart

Bonobos and chimpanzees remember familiar conspecifics for decades ↗

Bonobos and chimpanzees looked longer at photographs of former groupmates than strangers. These gaze patterns suggest that memories of social partners can endure for years, with evidence consistent with recognition after decades apart.

Authors and citation

Laura S. Lewis, Erin G. Wessling, Fumihiro Kano, Jeroen M. G. Stevens, Josep Call, Christopher Krupenye

Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 120(52), e2304903120. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2304903120 ↗

2022 · eLife

How the white of the eye shapes gaze visibility

Experimental evidence that uniformly white sclera enhances the visibility of eye-gaze direction in humans and chimpanzees ↗

Humans and chimpanzees judged gaze direction in eye images with manipulated contrast. The study experimentally tested how white sclera affects gaze visibility, connecting eye appearance with the evolution of communication.

Authors and citation

Fumihiro Kano, Yuri Kawaguchi, Yeow Hanling

eLife 11, e74086. DOI: 10.7554/eLife.74086 ↗

2024 · Journal of Experimental Biology

How crows switch between frontal and lateral vision

Gaze tracking of large-billed crows (Corvus macrorhynchos) in a motion capture system ↗

Visual-field measurements and 3D head tracking revealed how freely moving large-billed crows look at objects. They tended to use frontal binocular vision for moving targets and lateral vision for stationary ones, supporting a method for inferring attention from head orientation.

Authors and citation

Akihiro Itahara, Fumihiro Kano

Journal of Experimental Biology 227(6), jeb246514. DOI: 10.1242/jeb.246514 ↗

2013 · PLOS ONE

Tracking gaze while a chimpanzee moves and interacts

Head-Mounted Eye Tracking of a Chimpanzee under Naturalistic Conditions ↗

A lightweight head-mounted eye tracker recorded one female chimpanzee’s gaze as she moved and interacted face to face with a human. Her attention to faces, hands and food changed with the interaction, demonstrating a way to study gaze in more naturalistic social settings.

Authors and citation

Fumihiro Kano, Masaki Tomonaga

PLOS ONE 8(3), e59785. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0059785 ↗

2025 · Methods in Ecology and Evolution

A practical AI workflow for measuring behavior from video

YOLO-Behaviour: A simple, flexible framework to automatically quantify animal behaviours from videos ↗

This framework detects behaviors in video and turns them into measurements such as feeding or visit rates. Tests across birds, humans and large wild mammals show how accessible computer-vision methods can support behavioral observation.

Authors and citation

Alex Hoi Hang Chan, Prasetia Putra, Harald Schupp, Johanna Köchling, Jana Straßheim, Britta Renner, Julia Schroeder, William D. Pearse, Shinichi Nakagawa, Terry Burke, Michael Griesser, Andrea Meltzer, Saverio Lubrano, Fumihiro Kano

Bringing 3D behavior tracking into the field

Peering into the world of wild passerines with 3D-SOCS: Synchronized video capture and posture estimation ↗

Synchronized compact cameras reconstruct the postures and movements of wild small birds in 3D without tracking markers. Head orientation helps examine visual-field use, bringing fine-scale behavioral measurement into natural settings.
